Work / 05 Side project

Two motor drives,
one 99 mm board.

A dual VESC-compatible controller that packs two complete three-phase GaN inverters onto a single 99 × 99 mm board — with all three controllers sharing one internal CAN bus, so traction control is a local conversation instead of a cable.

Year

Role
Hardware design
Board

01The brief

Two-wheel-drive light electric vehicles — skateboards, e-bikes, small robots — usually carry two separate ESCs that have to agree with each other over a cable. Twin-GaN does the job with one board: two independent three-phase inverters, each with its own STM32F405, its own USB-C and its own three-shunt sensing chain, plus an ESP32-C3 as a third CAN node.

The bet is that integration beats modularity at this size. It is cheaper and smaller than the equivalent dual ESC, and the trade-off is deliberate: it gives up peak power for density.

03What makes it a system, not two boards

One CAN bus, three nodes. Both STM32s and the ESP32 are on the same internal bus with on-board termination, so the two channels can hand each other torque requests without an external harness. Traction control becomes a firmware feature rather than a wiring problem.

Two VESC targets, not one. Because each channel has its own MCU and its own 6-PWM stage, VESC Tool sees two separate controllers, identifies and configures them independently, and treats them exactly like two ESCs — which is what makes the board instantly usable by anyone who already knows the ecosystem.

Three-shunt sensing per channel with a real reference. One INA240A1D per phase across 2 mΩ shunts, with REF1 at GND and REF2 at 3V3, so the measurement is genuinely bidirectional and the firmware can tell forward from regen.

Every port accounted for. USB-C per controller with proper 5.1 kΩ CC pulldowns and a PTC on VBUS; Tag-Connect SWD per MCU; VESC accessory headers on the standard pinout. The details that make a board feel like a product instead of a prototype.

04Where it stands

R1 is drawn, and the numbers on this page are design targets — 60 V, 20 A battery current, 20 A continuous and 40 A absolute-max phase current per channel, roughly 1.2 kW total. None of it has been bench-verified. The repository carries the same warning at the top of the specification table, which is the only honest way to publish a board that has not yet been through a real load.
